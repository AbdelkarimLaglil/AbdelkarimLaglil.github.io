<h2>Topics in Comparative Politics</h2>
<p class="lead">Temas de Política Comparada, Universidad Carlos III de Madrid. Double Degree in Law and Political Science.</p>
<div class="lh-entry"><span class="title">Teaching Assistant, since September 2025</span><span class="who">Seminar groups 35 and 36, and two guest lectures (lecciones magistrales)</span></div>
<div class="lh-entry chapter"><span class="title">Student evaluations: 4.89 and 4.80 out of 5</span><span class="who">First semester of 2025/2026, anonymous surveys on a scale from 1 to 5, certified by the UC3M General Secretary on 5 February 2026</span></div>
<p>The course was designed and coordinated by <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/sebastianlavezzolo">Sebastián Lavezzolo</a>. Over fourteen weeks it moves from knowing content to understanding the argumentative logic of theories, with causal identification as a constant concern. It covers historical legacies, inequality and democratization, political regimes and their survival, state capacity, populism, electoral systems, institutions and accountability, and money in politics.</p>
<p>Several sessions connect to my research on far-right exclusionary politics and on violence. In the week on historical legacies, students read how the re-elaboration of fascism shaped right-wing populism in Western Europe, and how Nazi concentration camps left a legacy of out-group intolerance. Another week turns to populism and technocracy. Others connect to my work on remittances and political regimes.</p>

<section class="lh-dossier">
<h2>How I teach</h2>
<p class="lead">My seminars aim to move students from consuming research to producing it.</p>
<ul class="lh-questions">
<li><strong>Read analytically, not passively</strong><span>Every reading gets a one-page critical analysis: the question and argument, the methods and evidence, the contribution and limitations, and a critical reflection. Most of the words go to the last one, because that is what we debate in class.</span></li>
<li><strong>Start with the question, then match the design</strong><span>In workshops, students rebuild vague questions such as "Why do some governments work better than others?" until they are clear and researchable. Then they compare observational, quasi-experimental, and experimental designs: no design is best in general, and what matters is the fit with the question.</span></li>
<li><strong>Learn the language of the literature</strong><span>I wrote a Spanish–English glossary of quantitative terms for the course, from descriptive statistics to confounders and self-selection, so students can read published articles and spot weak evidence.</span></li>
<li><strong>Work in teams</strong><span>Students present articles, write analyses, and, if they wish, carry out the final project in pairs, because research in political science is mostly done in teams.</span></li>
</ul>
</section>

<h2>From question to conference talk</h2>
<p class="lead">Students build a group research project that reads like a journal article, 20 to 35 pages, quantitative or qualitative.</p>
<ul class="lh-timeline">
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 4: research question</span><span class="lh-where">Clear, specific, researchable, relevant, and original</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 5: theory and hypotheses</span><span class="lh-where">From a theory to an argument and falsifiable hypotheses</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 8: research design</span><span class="lh-where">Observational, quasi-experimental, and experimental designs, and levels of study</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 9: working with data</span><span class="lh-where">Hands-on session in Stata or R</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 12: recap</span><span class="lh-where">Coherence between question, theory, and design</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Weeks 13 and 14: conference-style presentations</span><span class="lh-where">Each group presents the whole project and receives feedback</span></li>
</ul>
