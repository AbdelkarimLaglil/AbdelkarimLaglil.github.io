<section class="lh-dossier">
<h2>How I teach</h2>
<p class="lead">My seminars aim to move students from consuming research to producing it.</p>
<ul class="lh-questions">
<li><strong>Read analytically, not passively</strong><span>Every reading gets a one-page critical analysis built around four questions: the question and argument, the methods and evidence, the contribution and limitations, and a critical reflection. Students spend most of their words on the last one, because that is what we debate in class.</span></li>
<li><strong>Solid research starts with a solid question</strong><span>In workshops, students take apart vague questions such as "Why do some governments work better than others?" and rebuild them until they are clear, specific, and researchable.</span></li>
<li><strong>Match the design to the question</strong><span>We compare observational, quasi-experimental, and experimental designs and the trade-off between internal and external validity. No design is best in general, and technical sophistication does not replace a good fit between question and method.</span></li>
<li><strong>Learn the language of the literature</strong><span>I wrote a Spanish–English glossary of quantitative terms for the course, from descriptive statistics to confounders, self-selection, and validity, so students can read published articles and spot weak evidence.</span></li>
<li><strong>Research is collaborative</strong><span>Students work in pairs to present articles, write critical analyses, and, if they wish, carry out the final project, because research in political science is mostly done in teams.</span></li>
</ul>
</section>

<section class="lh-dossier">
<h2>From question to conference talk</h2>
<p class="lead">Students build a group research project that reads like a journal article, 20 to 35 pages, quantitative or qualitative, in stages.</p>
<ul class="lh-timeline">
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 4: research question</span><span class="lh-where">Workshop on what makes a question clear, specific, researchable, relevant, and original</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 5: theory, mechanisms, and hypotheses</span><span class="lh-where">Workshop on how a theory leads to an argument and to falsifiable hypotheses</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 8: research design and operationalization</span><span class="lh-where">Workshop on observational, quasi-experimental, and experimental designs, and on micro, meso, and macro levels of study</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 9: working with data</span><span class="lh-where">Hands-on session in Stata or R, with one individual-level and one cross-country example</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Week 12: recap</span><span class="lh-where">Clarity of the question and the argument, coherence between theory and design, first ideas on data and analysis</span></li>
<li><span class="lh-venue">Weeks 13 and 14: conference-style presentations</span><span class="lh-where">Each group presents the whole project, as at a conference, and receives feedback</span></li>
</ul>
</section>

<section class="lh-dossier">
<h2>The course at a glance</h2>
<p class="lead">Temas de Política Comparada was designed and coordinated by <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/sebastianlavezzolo">Sebastián Lavezzolo</a>. I taught seminar groups 35 and 36.</p>
<p>The course goes from knowing content to understanding the argumentative logic of theories, with causal identification as a constant concern. Its fourteen weeks cover the scientific method, causality and identification, historical legacies (including those of political violence), inequality and democratization, the effects of political regimes, the survival of autocratic regimes, remittances and political regimes, state capacity, a data session, populism and technocracy, electoral systems and redistribution, political institutions and accountability, money in politics, and a final recap.</p>
<p>Two of those sessions overlap with my own research: the legacies of political violence, and remittances and political regimes.</p>
